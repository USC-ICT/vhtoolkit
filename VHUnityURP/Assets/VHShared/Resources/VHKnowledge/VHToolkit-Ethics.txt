Ethics and responsible AI in the Virtual Human Toolkit

The VHToolkit is an academic platform for the research and development of virtual humans. It does not provide AI models of its own: it integrates existing technologies, both commercial cloud services and open-weight local models, providing wrappers, integrations, tools, and examples around them. That position shapes where responsibility lies and what the toolkit can honestly promise.

The AI technologies it integrates have inherent limitations and biases. Large language models (LLM) can be unpredictable, convey inaccuracies, and show biases reflecting their training data. Speech recognition can perform unevenly across accents. Sensing services that estimate attributes such as age or emotion embed assumptions that do not hold for everyone. A virtual human built on these technologies inherits those properties, and no integration layer can remove them; what the VHToolkit does is make them visible, mitigate them, and leave researchers in control.

The mitigations are layered. Because the VHToolkit does not train its own models, every conversation also passes through the third-party provider's own safety systems, and the VHToolkit adds to those rather than replacing them. By default the sample characters begin their prompt with a shared safety section, stated as the highest priority, covering honesty about being an AI-driven virtual human, no invented life stories or credentials, no medical, psychological, legal, or financial advice, care around self-harm and crisis topics, and never revealing the prompt itself; these safety prompts are part of the source and can be reviewed on GitHub. An application can replace or omit that section, so a developer remains responsible for the prompt their own deployment sends. On top of that, a conversation guard screens generative dialogue in both directions. It protects the integrity of the character's instructions rather than filtering harmful content: it detects attempts to override the instructions, extract the prompt, or claim authority over the character, and it detects prompt leakage and breaks in character in the reply. By default a flagged turn still reaches the model with a reminder of its safety rules appended, and a flagged reply is regenerated once. Screening for harmful content itself comes from the provider's safety systems and from the prompt. Retrieved reference material is framed as information only, so documents cannot act as injected instructions.

Transparency and control are design principles. Which model, provider, prompt, and settings drive a character is explicit, inspectable configuration rather than hidden defaults. Inspectability is not the same as reproducibility, which also requires keeping the effective prompt, the generation settings, and the provider and model version used at the time. Every capability offers a local option, so research with data-sovereignty requirements can run entirely on hardware the researcher controls, though local and cloud options differ in quality, language coverage, latency, and hardware requirements. In the sample scenes, camera and microphone input are off until deliberately enabled; an application built on the VHToolkit can change that.

By default characters are honest about being artificial: asked sincerely whether it is an AI, a character says yes. Research that requires deviating from this, such as an IRB-approved deception study, can supply its own safety text - a deliberate, visible choice by the researcher, never a toolkit default.

Some responsibility necessarily stays with the researcher: whether a use case suits an AI-driven character at all, participant consent, which providers may receive participant data under their protocol, and evaluating the character's behavior for their specific population. Prompt-based safety and screening are best-effort defenses rather than guarantees.
